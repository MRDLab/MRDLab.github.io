Research Projects

Six active lines of research, spanning soft robotic hardware, smart surgical instruments, and the data science of surgical performance.

Additive manufacturing · Tissue engineering

3D Bioprinting Directly Onto Moving Human Anatomy

Organ transplantation has saved countless lives, but supply remains the limiting factor. Combining tissue engineering with additive manufacturing offers a route to building organ scaffolds directly — if the printer can cope with a target that will not hold still.

We investigate the possibility of 3D printing directly onto unpredictably moving human anatomy. The objective is to let surgeons print tissue onto other tissue — for example, printing a skin graft directly onto a patient without limiting the patient's range of motion. The core challenges are motion tracking, depositing material accurately on non-rigid moving surfaces, and trading print speed against print accuracy.

Previous researchers: Rebecca Smith, Reed Johnson, John O'Neill, Rodney Dockter, Anna French

Soft robotics · Inverse design

Soft Catheter Robots

The research objective of this work is to establish and validate a mathematical framework for the inverse design of universal soft robots: robots that deliver exceptional force and power densities, and that can be produced by robotic, automated manufacturing via 3D printed materials exploiting highly anisotropic elastomers, inextensible fibers, and beam elements.

The method is multiscale inverse design — generating actuator geometry fitted to a specific patient anatomy taken from CT. Anatomy centerlines are computed automatically via VMTK, convex blending maps the initial centerline to the desired one as a function of joint angle, and a Frenet–Serret parametrization fits the actuator surface to those centerlines.

Previous researchers: Mark Gilbertson, Gillian McDonald

Controls · Human–robot interaction

Shared Control

Despite the wealth of sensory data available during robot-assisted surgery, current surgical operations do not use that data. We are developing intelligent surgical robots that blend control inputs from both the robot and the surgeon, rather than treating the robot as a purely passive teleoperated tool.

A central focus is shared control of tissue grasping: holding the grasping force in an optimal range throughout a robotic procedure, even when the surgeon receives no force feedback at all.

Previous researchers: Trevor Stephens

Surgical data science · Machine learning

Surgical Skill Evaluation

How can technical surgical skill be quantified? We propose features for automated skill evaluation that stay relevant regardless of which surgical training task the tools are performing, and that can diagnose skill without requiring the complete time series of data from a given trial.

Open questions driving the work: how much does tool-motion trajectory tell us relative to full video? Which aspects of tool-motion dynamics separate skilled from unskilled performance? And what feedback most efficiently improves a trainee's ability? We also study the importance of pre-procedural warm-up in surgery, via the Robotic Readiness Study.

Previous researchers: Jason Kelly

Smart instruments · Sensing

Tissue Identification

Given the lack of force feedback available to clinicians performing laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgeries, it is often difficult to determine how much force to apply to a tissue — or even to tell which tissue is in the jaws.

We developed a laparoscopic grasper instrumented with force and displacement sensors that recognizes tissue in real time and relays that information back to the surgeon. Because measuring force directly at the grasping site is difficult, a parallel effort estimates grip force for da Vinci surgical tools from backend sensors using machine learning methods such as neural networks.

Previous researchers: Trevor Stephens, Rodney Dockter, Nathan Kong, Astrini Sie, Michael Winek

Biomechanics · Simulation

In-vivo and Ex-vivo Tissue Characterization

Accurate properties of living biological tissues are critically important to the advancement of medical simulation science. Two gaps stand in the way: there is no sufficient database of human tissue properties for surgical simulators to draw on, and there are few robotic tools able to measure in-vivo tissue characteristics in the first place.

We build both. A further thread examines how tissue properties shift between the living and ex-vivo states — accounting for perfusion, temperature, and related factors — so that the large body of existing ex-vivo data can be made useful for simulator development.

Contributors: Luis E. Morales Tenorio, Kelsey J. Devine, Jayme Lee, Timothy M. Kowalewski, Victor H. Barocas, Trevor K. Stephens, Kelsey A. Harper, Mark J. Brown
